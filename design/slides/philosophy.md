# Grazing Light

A design philosophy for the Penumbra title artwork (canvas-design skill, Nov 12).

**Light measured at its lowest angle.** The work lives in the thin band where light and darkness negotiate, a sliver of sky above a long edge. Nearly everything is darkness, so the darkness has to be composed, not left over. The black field is the material; light is a rare event in it, rationed and placed with painstaking attention so each lit mark carries the weight of a whole day.

**Space is horizontal and patient.** The composition is a long, low line that crosses the canvas and holds it. Above the line, the sky is a quiet instrument field: an even grid of faint points, like a plate in an observatory, there to be read against rather than looked at. Below it, form comes from accumulation. Thousands of fine strokes run in one direction, each one placed by hand. Their density, not any outline, describes the ground. The result should look as if it took weeks of careful drafting, the product of deep expertise in both drawing and measurement.

**Colour is a ration.** Black is the ground. A warm, near-white ink describes structure at very low strength, never shouting. One amber appears for light itself, only where light actually reaches: the source, its sharp rays and the few crests it touches. One cool blue marks a second, distant companion and its path, small and exact. No gradients and no glow. Light here is direct, hard-edged and scarce, and every colour decision is the result of meticulous calibration.

**Scale and rhythm come from repetition.** Marks repeat at a steady interval until the eye stops counting and starts seeing. The source is the one place where repetition breaks: rays of uneven length, cut cleanly by the edge so only half of it exists. Rhythm guides the eye from the vast dark field to the small lit event and back along the line. Master-level execution shows in the intervals. Nothing is approximate, and every gap is chosen.

**Text is a reference mark, not a message.** Words appear as a scientist annotates a plate: tiny monospaced coordinates, a scale with a few ticks, registration crosses in the corners. They whisper, sit on the grid and never compete with the image. The finished piece should read as an artifact from an imaginary discipline that studies the edge of light, meticulously crafted and quiet, rewarding anyone who looks longer.
